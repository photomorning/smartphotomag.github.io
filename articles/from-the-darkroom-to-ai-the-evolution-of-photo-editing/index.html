From the Darkroom to AI: The Evolution of Photo Editing

May 23, 2026 - 4 min read

Photo editing has come a long way. But what many modern photographers don't realize is that nearly every tool we use today — from dodging and burning to compositing and airbrushing — began in the chemical darkroom. From stacking glass negatives to telling an AI to "remove the background," this is the story of how photo editing evolved.

The Darkroom Era

Before computers, photographers manipulated light-sensitive paper with their hands. Dodging and burning were physical techniques — holding a small tool beneath the enlarger to withhold light from certain areas (dodging) or adding more exposure to others (burning). Contrast was controlled through paper grades and chemical baths, and composite images were created by stacking multiple negatives in the enlarger.

Cropping happened with an enlarger easel. Color toning involved selenium, sepia, and other chemical solutions. Every one of these techniques was slow, demanding, and entirely physical — but the creative results were stunning, and the fundamentals they established still define how we talk about editing today.

The Dawn of Digital

Photoshop's arrival in 1990 was the watershed moment. Suddenly, dodging and burning became a matter of brushing on a layer mask. Cropping required a drag of the mouse. Full compositing — layering elements from multiple images — became accessible to anyone with a computer.

Lightroom and Capture One soon followed, shifting the paradigm from destructive pixel editing to non-destructive raw processing. Photographers could now adjust exposure, white balance, and color globally or with gradients at any point in their workflow without degrading image quality.

The AI Revolution

Today, artificial intelligence has entered the editing suite with breathtaking speed. Tools like Adobe's Generative Fill, Skylum Luminar's AI Sky Replacement, and the content-aware features in modern software all trace their lineage back to darkroom techniques — but execute them in seconds rather than hours.

AI-driven noise reduction distinguishes image detail from sensor grain in real time. Auto-masking tools select subjects, skies, and even individual facial features with a click. AI culling tools analyze hundreds of images in minutes, selecting the sharpest frames with the best expressions.

What We've Kept, What We've Gained

The vocabulary of editing hasn't changed much in a century. We still dodge and burn, crop, adjust contrast, and retouch. What has changed is speed and precision. A complex composite that would have taken a darkroom master an entire day can now be built in a single sitting. An AI-assisted portrait retouch that looks natural and professional can be completed in seconds — though the highest-end work still benefits from a trained human eye.

Perhaps most importantly, modern tools preserve the original file. Non-destructive editing and powerful backup workflows mean photographers can experiment freely, knowing a single click or keystroke can return them to the untouched raw image.

Practical Takeaways for Today's Photographers

  • Learn the history. Understanding where editing techniques came from deepens your appreciation for the tools you use every day.
  • Experiment with AI tools as a starting point, not a final destination. Review and refine AI-generated edits to keep your personal touch.
  • Use non-destructive workflows. Whether you're editing in Lightroom, Capture One, or Photoshop with layers, always preserve the original file.

Final Thoughts

The history of photo editing is remarkably continuous. The core creative techniques are the same ones darkroom photographers used decades ago — just executed faster, with more precision, and without chemicals. Understanding that lineage doesn't just make you more knowledgeable; it makes you a more intentional editor.

FAQ

Did darkroom photographers really use dodging and burning? Yes. These are literal darkroom terms. Photographers used small tools on wires to block or add light to enlargements, lightening or darkening specific areas of the print.

When did Photoshop become widely used by photographers? Photoshop launched in 1990 and gained traction through the mid-1990s. By the early 2000s, it was the industry standard for professional photo editing across nearly all genres.

Can AI fully replace a human photo editor? Not yet. AI handles repetitive technical tasks with impressive speed, but creative decisions — mood, stylistic color grading, intentional composition — still require human judgment and taste.
